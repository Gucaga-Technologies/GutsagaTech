STAGE 11 · EXPANSION, CONSOLIDATION, AND MIGRATION OF FUNCTIONS

Expansion, consolidation, and migration of functions

At first, the difference is visible inside the company: a higher Availability Level, faster capital turnover, ordering and replenishment operating under common rules, and an assortment that changes with demand.

But what happens if these advantages operate simultaneously for many years?

They begin to reinforce one another.

Then the chain gains more than a better result at one pharmacy. It gains the ability to grow faster—provided that a new location does not undermine the quality of the model already built.

SIZE IS NOT MARKET POWER

Size alone does not guarantee durable market power. It can create negotiating leverage, but control of critical functions makes the position stronger and harder to replace.

If two chains are equally large, but one controls warehousing, purchasing, assortment, and customer access while the other depends on external players—are they equally powerful?

NEGOTIATING LEVERAGECONTROL OF CRITICAL FUNCTIONS115

THE ACCELERATION POINT

Build the machine first. Then step on the gas

Before a system is in place, growth often increases the need for money and management attention. Once the system exists, growth can create resources for the next stage of growth.

  • a high Availability Level retains customers;
  • inventory turnover releases capital;
  • automation reduces dependence on individual people;
  • a central warehouse can partially pool local uncertainty;
  • direct purchasing and private label can retain a larger share of margin within the chain;
  • redistribution releases capital from excess or

    inefficiently positioned inventory;

  • category management and network assortment meet more customer needs.

Scale before efficiency multiplies problems. Scale after efficiency can multiply advantage.

The key word is can. If the quality of the model declines, growth again becomes a source of complexity.

When the customer begins to do your work

WHAT REMAINS FOR THE DISTRIBUTOR?

A large chain may become progressively less dependent on the traditional distributor model: its own warehouse, internal logistics, direct contracts, and centralized inventory management change the role of the intermediary.

  • large volume and route density may decline;
  • part of the commercial margin may shrink;
  • the role of a mandatory intermediary may disappear.

But this is not a universal law. A distributor may move into retail, bring independent pharmacies together, become an infrastructure operator, or sell logistics, data, and specialized services.

The distributor's risk is losing not only volume, but also its former role.

HOW MANY BUYERS DETERMINE A MANUFACTURER’S ACCESS TO THE MARKET?

After consolidation, a significant share of customer traffic may be concentrated among a limited number of large chains. They influence assortment, availability, promotional visibility, competition with private label, and data on actual sales.

There may be many competitors on the manufacturing side. But access to the patient is often controlled by only a few large buyers.

Scale thus becomes control of access—but only where the chain actually controls a meaningful share of customer traffic.

CONSOLIDATION IS PRESSURE, NOT DESTINY

Must the market inevitably converge on a few large chains?

No. Economics can create pressure toward consolidation, but the final market structure is shaped by ownership rules, geography, reimbursement for medicines, concentration limits, the role of independent pharmacies, consumer culture, and public policy.

LARGE CHAINS

High concentration where scaling is permitted and efficient.

REGIONAL SYSTEMS

Consolidation proceeds by region and leaves several strong centers.

INDEPENDENTS + INFRASTRUCTURE

Ownership remains fragmented, while power concentrates in purchasing, logistics, or associations.

No scenario is inevitable. In one market, independent pharmacies give way to chains; in another, the law limits concentration and strengthens distributors, purchasing groups, or associations.

If a more efficient chain can scale while preserving the quality of its model, its advantage begins to reshape the market structure.

Market structure depends not only on the efficiency of players, but also on the rules under which they are allowed to grow.

Lost margin

A stockout is not just an operational problem. It is money the system could have earned but did not.

Potential margin = actual margin + estimated lost margin.
Share of lost margin = lost margin / potential margin × 100%.
In the book’s methodology: Availability Level = 100% − share of lost margin.

How to read it

Look at both the amount of the loss and the percentage. The trend matters more than a single period. The company-wide average hides the source of the problem—you need to drill down to specific items.

Key pair

Lost margin × days of inventory.

If the loss falls while inventory rises sharply, service may have been bought with excess capital.

What to ask in the meeting

  1. 1Which 10 items created the largest loss?
  2. 2Are these recurring or new problems?
  3. 3Where is the cause: forecast, order, lead time, or allocation?
  4. 4What rule do we change so the problem does not recur?
Typical mistakeDiscussing an “availability percentage” without translating it into money and causes.
Drill down to the cause in the same way: category → manager → supplier → location → SKU → cause → process change.

Days of inventory

Days of inventory is a simple way to express capital intensity. It shows how many days of current sales the average inventory can support.

Days of inventory = average inventory at purchase cost / average daily cost of goods sold.

How to read it

A reduction is useful only if lost margin does not rise alongside it. Otherwise, capital was released at the customer’s expense.

An increase is not always bad: a seasonal peak or supply risk may require a deliberate buffer. The question is whether we are managing it.

What to view alongside it

Lost margin—did we lose sales?

GMROI—does the inventory pay off?

Age—is all inventory moving?

Financing—who pays for the cycle?

What to ask in the meeting

  1. 1Where did days of inventory increase the most?
  2. 2What is the cause: demand, MOQ, discount, lead time, or replenishment?
  3. 3Do high inventory and high loss occur at the same time?
  4. 4Which items can be reduced without worsening service?

Work not with average inventory, but with exceptions: high inventory + low velocity; low inventory + high loss; inventory that does not match the actual lead time.

Two categories can generate the same margin while requiring very different amounts of capital. GMROI shows how much gross margin each €1 of average inventory generates.

100% = €1 of average inventory generates €1 of gross margin over 12 months.
GMROI trend

100% — over a year, capital generates gross margin equal to the average inventory invested. Above 100%—more margin per €1 of capital; below 100%—less.
Improvement

More margin with the same average inventory.

Improvement

The same result with lower average inventory.

False victory

High GMROI achieved through chronic stockouts.

Where it is especially useful

Category comparisons; large purchases “for the discount”; assortment decisions; supplier negotiations; slow-moving items.

Typical mistakeEvaluating purchasing only by the discount percentage. Unit price may improve while capital economics gets worse.

What to ask in the meeting

  1. 1Where does capital generate the least margin?
  2. 2Is the cause low margin or excessive inventory?
  3. 3Which discounts reduced GMROI because of purchase volume?
  4. 4What will happen to availability if this inventory is reduced?
The manager’s decision is not to “increase GMROI,” but to identify the cause: price/margin, inventory volume, demand, assortment, or supplier terms.

KPI 5 · FINANCING

Own-financing days

Two suppliers with the same price can have different costs for the business. This KPI shows how many days of the operating cycle the company finances with its own money.

22

days financed by us

illustration

Own-financing days = DIO + DSO − DPO.
DIO — days inventory outstanding · DSO — days sales outstanding (time waiting for customer payment) · DPO — days payable outstanding (supplier payment terms actually used). In finance, this is known as the cash conversion cycle (CCC).
Trend in own-financing days
JanFebMarAprMayJun
0 days — the cycle requires no company cash. A value below zero means external financing lasts longer than the cash cycle.

How to read it

Review it by supplier, category, and channel. The more positive days, the longer the company’s own capital remains tied up before returning as cash.

A reduction is good only if it was not bought by worsening availability, margin, or supply terms.

GMROI × financing

High GMROIStrong profileMargin is there, but our capital is required
Low GMROILower burden, weak product economicsWorst profile

Do not mix the formulas: GMROI shows margin per €1 of average inventory; this KPI shows how many days the money is actually ours.

What to ask in the meeting

  1. 1Where do we finance the cycle for the longest?
  2. 2What creates the days: DIO, DSO, or weak DPO?
  3. 3Do margin and GMROI compensate for this capital requirement?
  4. 4What is more profitable to change: batch size, frequency, customer payment, or supplier payment terms?
Typical mistakeTreating long payment terms as permission to buy excess product—or improving financing at the cost of stockouts.
The manager looks at the pair: GMROI — efficiency of inventory capital; own-financing days — how much of this capital the company actually finances.

Unwanted overstock is any quantity above what a location should hold, whether because demand has fallen, the product is being discontinued, or its expiration date leaves too little time to sell it. For this inventory, inaction is no longer acceptable: every item needs a specific decision, owner, and deadline.

The criterion must be formalized: inventory above the defined limit; no movement; age above the threshold; expiration risk; discontinued product; inventory does not match demand.

ONE SUPPLY CHAIN

One product flow should have one end-to-end owner

The supplier, central warehouse, distribution, and pharmacy may sit in different departments. But for the customer, there is only one outcome—the product is either available or it is not. If everyone is responsible only for their own segment, the cause of the loss falls between departments.

WHO SHOULD SEE THIS ENTIRE CHAIN?

ONE SUPPLY CHAIN MANAGEMENT FUNCTION

The name may differ: supply chain, inventory and replenishment management, planning and logistics. The point is not the name, but end-to-end responsibility for planning, ordering, replenishment, distribution, inventory movement, and Availability Level—the ability to ensure that the customer has the required product when it is needed—from supplier to point of sale.

CATEGORY MANAGER

Assortment, commercial terms, price, the role of the category, and the decision about what the company wants to sell.

END-TO-END SUPPLY CHAIN FUNCTION

To ensure that the selected assortment is physically available: the right inventory, in the right place, at the right time, with a clear cause identified for every failure.

The product was unavailable. Why? The delivery arrived late. Why? The order was triggered late. Why? The replenishment parameter did not reflect the actual lead time.
